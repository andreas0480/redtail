Common Redstart

Phoenicurus phoenicurus · Rödstjärt

The Common Redstart is a small migratory passerine of the Old World flycatcher family Muscicapidae. Its name comes from the archaic English word start — tail — and the Greek phoinix (crimson). The constant quivering of that bright orange-red tail is the bird's most distinctive field character.

Widespread across Europe and western Asia in summer, the redstart is a long-distance migrant that spends its winters in the Sahel belt of West Africa. It favours old, open woodland with natural cavities — and readily adopts nest boxes, which is how this individual came to be so closely watched.

Appearance

Male

Unmistakable in breeding plumage. The crown, nape, and mantle are slate blue-grey; the forehead bears a white blaze. Face and throat are solid black. Breast and flanks are vivid orange-rufous, grading paler towards the vent. The rump and most of the tail are the diagnostic bright orange-red — the central pair of tail feathers alone is dark brown. In fresh autumn plumage narrow pale feather fringes give a somewhat washed-out look; these abrade away by spring to reveal full breeding colours without a moult.

Female

Considerably less striking than the male but still distinctive once known. Grey-brown above with a neat white eye-ring; underparts creamy-buff with a faint orange wash. The rump and tail match the male's orange-red coloration and are the key identification feature at a distance. Older females occasionally show a faint dark bib. Juveniles of both sexes are brown with buffy-white spotting overall, similar to young Robins, but always show the orange tail.

Distribution & Habitat

The Common Redstart breeds across the Palearctic from Morocco and Iberia east through virtually all of Europe and temperate Asia to Lake Baikal. It is absent from Iceland and very local in Ireland. In Sweden it is a common and widespread summer visitor — one of the characteristic sounds of birch and mixed woodland from May onwards.

It is strongly associated with open, mature woodland with high horizontal visibility and a low, sparse understorey: old oak and birch forest, traditional orchard "high-stem meadow" landscapes, riverine alder and willow stands, and parkland with ancient trees. Key habitat features are an abundance of old trees with natural cavities (or nest boxes), short grass or bare ground for foraging, and standing dead wood as song posts. It avoids dense, structurally uniform plantations.

Wintering birds occupy the Sahel belt of West Africa, roughly 5–20°N, from Senegal and The Gambia east through Mali and Burkina Faso. Geolocator studies on Danish-breeding birds show winter sites concentrated around southern Mali and northern Burkina Faso. Individual birds are largely sedentary within their winter territory despite the highly seasonal Sahelian climate.

Migration

The Common Redstart is one of the longer-distance European migrants, covering roughly 5,000–6,000 km between breeding and wintering grounds. Spring and autumn routes form a pronounced loop.

Spring (northbound): Birds leave Africa from late February, moving broadly north through Iberia and then fanning north-east across France and central Europe towards Scandinavia. Males arrive on breeding grounds 3–5 days ahead of females. In Sweden the first males typically appear in late April, with most birds arriving in early May — among the later arrivals of European summer visitors.

Autumn (southbound): One of the earliest migrants to depart. Scandinavian breeders leave from mid-July. The autumn route loops counterclockwise: south-west through western Europe and down the Atlantic coast of Iberia and Morocco, then a sharp eastward turn at the Saharan edge into the Sahel interior — a strikingly different path from the direct spring return. Falsterbo at the southern tip of Sweden is a well-known bottleneck where hundreds of redstarts can be logged in a single August morning.

Breeding Biology

Territory & courtship

Males establish territories of roughly 0.5–1 ha, singing from exposed perches at dawn and dusk. The song is a soft, melancholy warble in three parts: clear fluted introduction, a repetitive middle section, and a variable, sometimes jangling finale. Territories are defended vigorously in the first weeks after arrival.

The male's courtship display is one of the most characteristic behaviours: he approaches the female with wings raised and rapidly trembling ("wing-shivering"), tail fanned and dipped to expose the full orange-red rump and outer rectrices. He also leads her to candidate nest sites, repeatedly entering and singing from the entrance. The female makes the final site choice.

Nest building

Only the female builds, taking 1.5–8 days (mean ~3.6 days for first broods). The outer cup uses dry grass, plant stems, roots, bark strips, moss, and leaves; the inner cup is lined with finer hair, wool, and feathers. One study recorded at least 600 material-carrying trips for a single nest.

Natural nest sites are tree cavities, woodpecker holes, crevices in stone walls, and hollow roots. The species readily accepts enclosed nest boxes with an oval entrance (48×32 mm preferred; round 40–45 mm accepted), floor ~130×130 mm, and depth ~170–200 mm, placed at 1.5–4 m in shade with the entrance facing north to south-east.

Eggs & laying

Eggs are uniform pale sky-blue, essentially unspeckled — among the cleanest-coloured eggs of any European passerine. Dimensions approximately 19×14 mm, weighing ~1.9 g (about 11–13% of female body mass — relatively large). Clutch size averages 6.3 eggs for first broods (range 2–10 recorded); laying begins in late May to early June in Sweden and northern populations, somewhat earlier in central Europe.

One egg is laid per day, always in the morning. Incubation typically does not begin until the last or penultimate egg is laid, ensuring largely synchronous hatching. Northern populations such as in Sweden are usually single-brooded; the short summer does not permit a second clutch.

Incubation

The female incubates alone for 12–14 days (mean 12.95 days). She spends roughly 75% of daylight hours on the nest, leaving for foraging breaks totalling about 25% of the day. Cavity nests retain heat well, buffering brief absences. The male does not incubate but remains highly active on the territory: singing from perches near the nest, maintaining the territorial boundary, and occasionally bringing food to the sitting female.

Chick development & fledging

Hatching is largely synchronous because incubation began with the full clutch. Chicks are altricial: blind, with sparse dark grey down, utterly helpless at hatching. The female broods almost continuously for the first five days. Both parents then share provisioning at increasing rates — up to ~500 feeds per day in the final days before fledging — delivering soft caterpillars, small flies, beetles, and spiders.

Fledging occurs at 12–17 days (mean ~16 days), usually early in the morning. Fledglings remain dependent on the parents for a further 7–14 days. As in many songbirds, the male often takes over care of the independent fledglings while the female begins a replacement or second clutch in central European populations.

Behaviour

Tail-quivering

Both sexes continuously quiver the orange tail — a rapid up-and-down trembling that continues during perching, singing, foraging, and alarm. This is the most immediately obvious field character. It is thought to function in communication and may also flush invertebrate prey from vegetation.

Foraging

A classic perch-and-sally predator: it watches from a low branch, stone, or post then drops to bare ground or short turf to snatch a prey item, sometimes pursuing flying insects aerially like a flycatcher. It rarely forages in dense vegetation and strongly prefers open ground with good visibility. Prey includes flies, caterpillars, beetles, ants, spiders, and earthworms; berries and small fruits are important fuel during autumn migration.

Cuckoo parasitism

The Common Redstart is a regular host of the Common Cuckoo in some European populations. Unusually, studies show that redstart chicks do not suffer markedly increased mortality when sharing a nest with a cuckoo chick — partly because the large cuckoo helps maintain nest temperature. This makes the redstart a notably tolerant host compared to many other cuckoo victims.

Conservation

The European population as a whole has increased modestly since 1980, but regional trends diverge sharply. The UK has seen a long-term decline of ~10% since 1967 and a range contraction of 31%, leading to Amber listing. Central European populations have also fallen in some regions.

The principal threats are: climate-driven drought in Sahelian wintering grounds (poor Sahel rainfall correlates directly with European breeding declines the following year); loss of traditional orchards and veteran trees with natural cavities; agricultural intensification raising grass height and reducing foraging ground; and pesticide use reducing insect prey. Nest box schemes and the retention of dead wood and old trees are among the most effective conservation interventions.
